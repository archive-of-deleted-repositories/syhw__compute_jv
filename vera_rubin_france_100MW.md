# Vera Rubin AI Factory Plan for France

## Bottom line

The strongest public-evidence path is to separate the program into two layers: a near-term **Vera Rubin NVL72 buildout** that uses today’s liquid-cooled AC facility designs, and a later **800 VDC-ready Rubin Ultra / Kyber-scale expansion path** that is only worth committing to once NVIDIA’s 2027-generation electrical and rack standards are fully frozen. NVIDIA’s public roadmap supports this split: Vera Rubin NVL72 is already ramping into full production for second-half 2026 deployments, while NVIDIA’s 800 VDC architecture is explicitly tied to **Kyber-era** megawatt-class racks and the 2027 rollout. citeturn37view0turn8view2turn8view3turn33view2

For **site choice**, Dunkerque ZGI is the best technical fit if the goal is to minimize logistics and utility risk for a very large AI factory: it has a public port/industrial setting, direct road/rail/port access, drinking and industrial water nearby, fiber and gas proximity, and a documented RTE path to a **secure ~700 MW** direct 400 kV underground connection, plus discussions around a **temporary 30–40 MW** provisional feed that could support a first tranche. Le Bosquel is extremely strong on headline power timing — public materials describe **240 MW in 24 months, 1 GW in 4 years, and possible extension to 1.4 GW** — but it is no longer a clean greenfield opportunity in public terms because CC2SO says the site was selected for a consortium involving **SoftBank and Sesterce**, and SoftBank’s current French plan explicitly includes Bosquel in its first-phase portfolio. citeturn20view1turn27view0turn25view0turn23view0turn22search10turn44search1

My recommended order of operations is therefore: **Dunkerque first if you need an independently actionable site**, **Bosquel only via a JV / partnership / carve-out with the current site winner**, and **former PSA Aulnay-sous-Bois only as a speculative brownfield branch** because it has substantial land but no publicly documented fast-track 100 MW+ intake. The former PSA site is large and already sees opportunistic reuse, including logistics, a data center, a geothermal plant, and future Grand Paris infrastructure, but that same evidence also shows the site is fragmented and already being repurposed rather than sitting as a clean, utility-ready campus. citeturn17view0turn20view1turn25view0turn23view0

## What the public Vera Rubin roadmap really supports

The official NVIDIA product you want for the first build is **Vera Rubin NVL72**. NVIDIA describes it as a rack-scale AI supercomputer with **72 Rubin GPUs, 36 Vera CPUs, sixth-generation NVLink, ConnectX-9 SuperNICs, and BlueField-4 DPUs**. Public preliminary specs list **3,600 PFLOPS NVFP4 inference**, **20.7 TB of HBM4**, **54 TB of LPDDR5X CPU memory**, and **28.8 TB/s scale-out networking bandwidth** per rack. NVIDIA also states that Vera Rubin NVL72 is in production scale-up now, supported by a large MGX ecosystem, and HPE says its **Vera Rubin NVL72 by HPE** rack-scale system will be available in **December 2026**. citeturn8view2turn11view0turn37view0turn42view0

The cooling and serviceability story is unusually favorable for phased deployment. NVIDIA says the third-generation MGX rack can be **100% liquid-cooled**, uses **45°C liquid cooling**, has **liquid-cooled busbars**, and supports **de-ionized water or PG25** in a closed loop. The Vera Rubin NVL72 compute tray also moves to a cable-free, hose-free, fanless tray design, and NVIDIA says compute tray assembly drops from roughly **two hours to five minutes**, which materially helps ramp-up, sparing, and field replacement planning. citeturn12view0turn14view0

The part that needs caution is the user shorthand **“Kyber NVL576.”** Public NVIDIA materials do **not** describe Kyber as the 576-GPU product name. Officially, **Vera Rubin Ultra NVL576** is an eight-rack, 576-GPU NVLink domain built from separate MGX NVL racks with copper plus direct optical connections. **Kyber** is the next rack architecture after Oberon/MGX NVL, and NVIDIA says it first arrives as a **standalone NVL144** and later scales further into **NVL1152**. In other words: the public 576-GPU object is Rubin Ultra NVL576; the public Kyber object is the next rack generation that enables even denser future systems. citeturn12view0turn14view0

That distinction matters because the **electrical architecture also bifurcates**. NVIDIA’s public 800 VDC roadmap is not a requirement for the first Vera Rubin NVL72 tranches. Instead, NVIDIA says the move to **800 VDC** is aimed at **1 MW IT racks and beyond**, with full-scale production aligned to **Kyber in 2027**. The practical consequence is simple: **build Oberon/Vera Rubin tranches with today’s proven AC-fed liquid-cooled facility standards, but make the campus 800 VDC-ready from day one** with reserved electrical rooms, cable paths, converter spaces, and protection architecture for the later Kyber-era transition. citeturn8view3turn33view2turn7search3

## Tranche designs that scale

For the **Oberon option**, I would define the tranche as **20–25 MW gross facility load**, not by rack count. Using a planning assumption of **PUE 1.15** for a warm-water liquid-cooled AI hall and roughly **150 kW IT per NVL72 rack** — directionally consistent with Schneider’s GB300 reference design up to **142 kW per rack** and current reporting that Rubin-class racks are around **150 kW or higher** — a 20–25 MW gross tranche yields about **17–22 MW of IT**, or roughly **116–145 Vera Rubin NVL72 racks**. That corresponds to about **8,352–10,440 GPUs** per tranche. I would package each tranche as a self-contained building block with its own CDU farm, medium-voltage plant, network spine, acceptance environment, and sparing stock. citeturn33view0turn32news45

For the **100 MW gross Oberon campus**, the same planning logic yields roughly **87 MW IT**, or around **580 NVL72 racks**, equivalent to roughly **41,760 GPUs**. This is large enough that the right operational unit is no longer the “data hall,” but the **repeatable availability block**: think independent 20–25 MW halls with separate commissioning and risk acceptance, ringed by a common utility yard and optical backbone. That structure matches the way NVIDIA, Vertiv, and Schneider are all talking about repeatable reference designs and infrastructure building blocks rather than one-off custom rooms. citeturn33view1turn33view0turn37view0

For the **Kyber-scale option**, I would not commit to a public rack-count promise yet, because NVIDIA has not publicly frozen the commercial per-rack power/BOM details for the Kyber generation. Instead, I would define the tranche by **electrical block size**, with **two 12.5 MW modular infrastructure blocks** forming a 25 MW tranche and **eight 12.5 MW blocks** forming a 100 MW campus. That maps cleanly to Vertiv’s public **OneCore Rubin DSX** architecture and lines up with NVIDIA’s 800 VDC direction for megawatt-class racks. Inside those blocks, the first 2027–2028 deployments should be designed around **Rubin Ultra NVL576 domains** and only upgraded to fuller Kyber density once NVIDIA’s commercial and safety stack is final. citeturn33view1turn8view3turn12view0

The key design principle is therefore: **Oberon tranches should optimize time-to-first-token; Kyber tranches should optimize future TCO and density.** NVIDIA says 800 VDC could improve end-to-end efficiency by up to **5%**, reduce maintenance costs by up to **70%**, and cut overall TCO by up to **30%** in the next rack generation. That is compelling enough to reserve the future path, but not compelling enough to delay a 2026–2027 Rubin NVL72 tranche that can be built with far less execution risk. citeturn8view3turn33view2

## Provider stack to line up now

For the **data center EPC / owner’s engineer layer**, the highest-confidence French and European names are **CAP INGELEC**, **Bouygues Construction**, **Eiffage**, and **Equans**. CAP INGELEC is the most obviously data-center-specialized of the four: it positions itself as a French leader in turnkey data center delivery, emphasizes energy performance and PUE control, and lists references such as Data4, Equinix, Telehouse, Interxion, and CNRS-IN2P3. Bouygues says it has delivered **more than 800 MW** of data center capacity and offers turnkey delivery from design to commissioning. Eiffage has public references both for very large French data center construction and for cooling plants on the Data4 Marcoussis campus through Clévia. Equans positions itself across the full data center value chain, including construction, HVAC, electrical connection, controls, maintenance, and heat recovery. citeturn30view0turn30view1turn30view2turn30view3turn31search0turn31search6

The sea leg will still add a multi-week ocean transport window and customs / drayage steps, whereas a Europe-built system shifts more of the critical path to truck scheduling and site acceptance. ## How the sites compare

**Le Bosquel** is the speed-to-power story. The CC2SO and associated public AMI material describe **33 hectares immediately available**, **17 hectares of additional extension potential**, and a fast-track power path of **240 MW in 24 months**, **1 GW in 4 years**, with possible extension to **1.4 GW**. CC2SO also now says the site was chosen for a consortium involving **SoftBank and Sesterce**, and SoftBank’s current French AI plan includes Bosquel in its first phase. That means Bosquel is no longer best treated as an independently actionable open site; it is best treated as a **partnering or carve-out opportunity** if you can work with the current site winner. In pure electrical timing terms, though, public evidence suggests it is one of the fastest serious AI-factory sites in France. citeturn25view0turn23view0turn22search10turn44search1

The right **Bosquel playbook** is therefore: do **not** treat it as a fresh competitive site acquisition. Instead, treat it as a **JV / powered-land / secondary-building / black-building** negotiation with the current sponsor group. If access is obtainable, Bosquel is very well suited to an **Oberon-first** plan because its public power timeline is already aligned with 20–25 MW self-contained tranches that can begin as soon as the current consortium is ready to open infrastructure packages or powered-shell parcels. The biggest question at Bosquel is no longer “is there enough grid?” but “what exactly is left available to a second entrant, and on what commercial terms?” citeturn25view0turn23view0turn44search1

**Dunkerque ZGI** is the utility-rich, logistics-rich, industrial-scale story. The port says the ZGI platform is turnkey, industrially zoned, and in the immediate vicinity of **drinking water, industrial water, gas, and optic fibre**, with direct road, rail, and port access. The current AMI for the specific data center/supercomputer parcel describes around **21 hectares**, and RTE’s site note says the definitive connection path is a direct underground tie to the **Flandres Maritimes** substation under construction, about **3 km away**, using a **double 400 kV underground link** capable of securely supplying about **700 MW**. RTE also says it is discussing a **provisional 30–40 MW** first power step through the Grand Port source station. citeturn27view0turn20view0turn20view1

The right **Dunkerque playbook** is therefore a staged one. Use the **provisional 30–40 MW** if it is contractually available to launch a **20–25 MW gross Oberon tranche**, while simultaneously building the long-life MV/HV yards, water loops, and network backbone for the definitive **400 kV / 700 MW** connection. Dunkerque is also the better home for the **Kyber-scale** branch because the port-industrial environment, water availability, and multimodal logistics are more naturally suited to megawatt-rack evolution and imported modular systems. Even beyond the specific data center parcel, RTE’s wider Dunkirk planning assumes **3,500 MW by 2030** and **4,500 MW by 2040** of additional electricity consumption on the territory, which is about as strong a public sign as you can get that the grid operator expects very large industrial loads there. citeturn20view1turn28view0

For the **deprecated automotive factory option**, the best public candidate is the **former PSA Aulnay-sous-Bois** site. URBASTUDIO says the former PSA site covers **175 hectares** and is one of the largest redevelopable land reserves in northern Greater Paris, but also notes that it has already been progressively reoccupied by **logistics, a data center, a geothermal plant, and future Grand Paris infrastructure**. That makes it a usable strategic brownfield only if the commercial reality is favorable and you can still assemble a large enough contiguous parcel while also securing a new dedicated 225 kV/400 kV supply path. Public evidence today does **not** show a Bosquel- or Dunkerque-like fast-track >100 MW intake, so I would keep Aulnay as an opportunistic branch, not as the baseline plan. citeturn17view0

## TCO and financing

The first TCO point is conceptual: **don’t confuse campus CapEx with fully populated compute CapEx**. SemiAnalysis’ ClusterMAX work argues that GPU-hour prices hide meaningful cost in networking, storage, reliability, setup, support, and goodput losses, while current third-party analyst estimates for **Vera Rubin NVL72** suggest the system itself is already extraordinarily expensive, before the campus around it is finished. Current non-official analyst estimates cluster around roughly **$7.8 million per NVL72 rack**, with some forecasts closer to **$9.1 million** as HBM4 pricing rises. Those are not NVIDIA list prices, but they are directionally useful for board-level planning. citeturn40search3turn40search11turn41search0turn41news29

Using those third-party rack estimates, a **20 MW gross Oberon tranche** at the planning densities above implies roughly **116 racks**, or about **$0.9–1.1 billion** of NVL72 rack spend alone. A **25 MW gross tranche** implies roughly **145 racks**, or about **$1.1–1.3 billion** of NVL72 rack spend alone. A **100 MW gross Oberon campus** implies around **580 racks**, or roughly **$4.5–5.3 billion** of NVL72 rack spend alone. Those figures exclude land, buildings, transformers, utility yards, CDUs, pumps, dry coolers, storage racks, LPX inference racks, software, taxes, customs, and financing costs, so they should be treated as **compute-rack-only planning envelopes** rather than all-in program budgets. citeturn41search0turn41news29turn33view0

For annual energy OPEX, a **planning assumption** is the right way to model rather than pretending to know the future tariff. At **20 MW gross**, annual electricity consumption is about **175 GWh**; at **25 MW**, about **219 GWh**; at **100 MW**, about **876 GWh**. If you model contracted power at **€50–80/MWh** as a scenario range, that yields about **€8.8–14.0 million per year** for 20 MW, **€11.0–17.5 million per year** for 25 MW, and **€43.8–70.1 million per year** for 100 MW, before water-treatment, staffing, maintenance, and network transit. Those are model assumptions, not quoted tariffs, but they are directionally appropriate for investment screening. NVIDIA’s public 800 VDC roadmap matters here because the company believes the next-generation architecture can cut end-to-end TCO by up to **30%** once the megawatt-rack model is truly in production. citeturn8view3turn33view2

On **financing structure**, the most sensible approach is a **split-capital-stack model**. European data center finance is now mature enough to support bridge loans, project finance, portfolio debt, and debt-capital-markets solutions, while law firms tracking the sector note that financing appetite has expanded with the growth of AI loads. A recent French AI campus example reported by the *Financial Times* used roughly **one-third equity and two-thirds debt** for a **500 MW** program, which is a good anchor for infrastructure-only thinking even if your IT financing strategy differs. Climate-finance groups are also explicitly pushing **green bonds** and **sustainability-linked loans** for data centers meeting credible standards. citeturn43search11turn43search1turn43news45turn43search5turn43search9

For a **20–25 MW tranche**, I would finance the deal in three layers. First, put the **land, grid connection, shell, and mechanical/electrical plant** into a project SPV funded with roughly **30–40% sponsor equity** and **60–70% senior construction / infrastructure debt**. Second, finance the **compute layer separately** through either equipment leasing / vendor-backed financing / asset-backed debt or through signed capacity pre-commitments with anchor users. Third, retain a working-capital / contingency line for spares, software, and acceptance overruns. That structure limits the amount of sponsor equity sitting idle in uninstalled GPUs and makes each tranche refinanceable once it reaches stable operations. The reason to keep hardware outside the pure real-estate SPV is that compute depreciation and refresh cycles are much shorter than grid and shell assets. citeturn43search11turn43search1turn40search3

For a **100 MW campus**, the financing should be staged, not monolithic. The cleanest plan is **four 25 MW tranches** or **five 20 MW tranches**, each with its own mechanical completion, energization, commissioning, and revenue start. Fund the master utility yard and the first two blocks up front, then refinance the stabilized infrastructure with longer-dated infra debt or bond proceeds as additional blocks are turned on. This is exactly where Dunkerque’s provisional 30–40 MW discussion becomes commercially useful: it gives you a chance to prove absorption and operational performance before the full 400 kV campus connection is complete. citeturn20view1turn43search11turn43search1

## Delivery path and limitations

The near-term **delivery path** should be straightforward. Reserve OEM/ODM production slots now, complete owner’s requirements and digital-twin models in 2026, freeze the first 20–25 MW tranche around **Vera Rubin NVL72**, and keep the campus masterplan **800 VDC-ready** without waiting for Kyber’s final commercial freeze. On the OEM side, NVIDIA says Vera Rubin is in full production scale-up now, HPE says its Rubin NVL72 system arrives in **December 2026**, and the ecosystem includes all the expected Taiwan builders. A practical reading of those sources is that **late 2027 to 2028** is the realistic first window for a fully commissioned French Rubin hall if grid, permits, and long-lead MEP are started immediately. The Kyber-scale branch should be treated as a **phase-two path**, likely **2028+** in France once the 2027 generation is truly deployment-ready at scale. citeturn37view0turn42view0turn33view2

The most actionable recommendation is therefore this. **If you need a project you can actually advance now, run Dunkerque as the primary program**: first tranche = **20–25 MW gross, Vera Rubin NVL72, AC-fed liquid cooling, 800 VDC-ready**, with **CAP INGELEC + Bouygues/Eiffage** on EPC, **Vertiv and/or Schneider** on power/cooling reference design, and **HPE Europe** or **Wiwynn/QCT/Foxconn Taiwan** as the rack-integration path depending whether you optimize for truck speed or ODM cost. **Run Bosquel as a strategic parallel discussion**, but only through the current site winner. **Keep Aulnay as a speculative brownfield option**, not a core delivery assumption. citeturn20view1turn27view0turn23view0turn42view0turn37view0turn30view0turn30view1turn30view2turn33view0turn33view1

The main limitations are four. First, **Kyber commercial power density, rack BOM, and deployment packaging are not yet fully public**, so any exact Kyber rack-count model would be false precision.
